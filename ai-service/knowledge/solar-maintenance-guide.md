# Solar PV Maintenance Guide

## Electrical safety before any work

Solar modules produce DC voltage whenever light reaches them, even on cloudy days, and
they cannot be switched off at the panel. Before any electrical work: switch off the AC
isolator, then the DC isolator, and wait for the time stated in the inverter manual so
its capacitors can discharge. Apply lock-out/tag-out so nobody switches the system back
on. Always verify with a voltmeter that circuits are dead. Wear insulated gloves and eye
protection. Never disconnect a DC connector under load, because DC arcs do not
extinguish by themselves. Only qualified technicians may open combiner boxes or replace
fuses.

## Low energy output: first checks

When a system produces much less energy than expected, check the simple causes first:
1. Weather: heavy cloud or rain lowers output. Compare with the expected energy, which
   is already based on the weather forecast.
2. Inverter status: look for fault codes, warnings or a "grid fault" message on the
   inverter display.
3. Monitoring: make sure readings arrived for the whole day. Missing readings are not
   the same as zero production.
4. Shading: new obstacles such as tree growth or construction.
5. Soiling: dust, bird droppings or leaves on the modules.
If output is low on one inverter only, the cause is usually local to that inverter or
its strings.

## Inverter shows very low output or is offline

If an inverter produces almost nothing, check whether it is switched on and connected
to the grid. Common causes are a tripped AC breaker, an open DC isolator, a grid
voltage or frequency fault that made the inverter disconnect for safety, an
insulation (isolation) fault, or an internal fault code. Write down the exact error code
and look it up in the manufacturer's manual. Many grid faults clear by themselves once
the grid is stable. Repeated faults need a technician.

## String-level problems

A large inverter usually has several strings or MPPT inputs. If one string produces far
less current than the others under the same sunlight, possible causes are a blown
string fuse, a loose or burnt connector, a damaged cable, or a failed module or bypass
diode. Compare string currents at the same time of day. An IV curve tracer helps a
technician find the faulty part.

## High inverter temperature and derating

Inverters reduce their output power when they get too hot, which is called thermal
derating. This protects the electronics but lowers energy production. Typical causes
are blocked ventilation openings, dust on heat sinks, failed cooling fans, or direct
sun on the inverter. Keep the space around the inverter free, clean the air paths, and
check that the fans run. Repeated over-temperature alerts mean the installation or
cooling needs to be inspected.

## Voltage out of range

Grid-connected inverters must disconnect when the grid voltage is too high or too low.
Frequent over-voltage often happens at the end of long cable runs or when many solar
systems feed the same local grid. Under-voltage can come from weak grid connections. If
voltage alerts repeat at the same site, measure the voltage at the connection point and
contact the grid operator. Do not change inverter grid settings without permission from
the grid operator.

## Module cleaning

Dirty modules can lose several percent of their output, and more in dusty areas. Clean
in the early morning or evening when modules are cool, because cold water on hot glass
can cause thermal stress. Use soft brushes and clean water, ideally demineralised. Do
not use high-pressure washers, abrasive tools or strong detergents, because they damage
the anti-reflective coating and seals. Never walk on modules. Compare output before and
after cleaning to decide how often cleaning is worth it.

## Hot spots and thermal imaging

A hot spot is a part of a module that becomes much hotter than the rest, caused for
example by a cracked cell, a failed bypass diode or partial shading. Hot spots reduce
output and can damage the module. A thermal (infrared) camera shows them clearly when
the system works under strong sunlight. Modules with severe hot spots should be checked
by a technician and may need replacement.

## Preventive maintenance schedule

A typical preventive plan includes: daily review of monitoring and alerts; monthly
visual inspection for damage, shading and soiling; cleaning as needed; checking
inverter fans and ventilation every few months; and a yearly inspection by a qualified
technician with torque checks on connections, insulation resistance tests and thermal
imaging. Record every inspection so problems can be compared over time.

## Normal ageing of modules

Solar modules slowly lose output over the years, typically about half a percent per
year. This normal ageing is gradual. A sudden drop is never normal ageing and points to
a fault, soiling or shading.
